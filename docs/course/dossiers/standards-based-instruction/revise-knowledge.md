# Helping Students Revise Their Knowledge

**Domain:** Standards-Based Instruction  
**Current status:** Publicly verified element name and placement in extending thinking/analysis  
**Primary sources:** `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-FTEM-CURRENT`

## Verified public-source claims

Current MEC material places **Helping Students Revise Their Knowledge** in the *extending thinking / analysis* group of Standards-Based Instruction.

The current public material confirms the element's identity and location in the cognitive-demand progression, but it does not provide enough protocol-level detail for this repository to state the exact current focus statement or desired effect.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

Learning should change what a student thinks, not merely add another fact to a notebook. Revising knowledge means learners **update, refine, reorganize, qualify, or replace an earlier understanding** in response to new evidence, stronger reasoning, feedback, or a better model.

The important evidence is visible change between an earlier and later conception.

## Strong evidence

Useful evidence may include students:

- correcting an earlier misconception and explaining why it changed;
- revising a model, explanation, prediction, or claim after new evidence;
- adding qualifications to an overly broad rule;
- reorganizing a concept map to reflect a more accurate relationship;
- comparing an initial answer with a later answer and explaining the difference;
- replacing a weak strategy with a better-supported one.

## False positives

Do not automatically count these as knowledge revision:

- erasing a wrong answer after the teacher supplies the correct one;
- copying corrections from the board;
- editing grammar or formatting without changing understanding;
- changing an answer without being able to explain why;
- completing a second attempt that happens to be correct by guessing.

## Common confusion

### Revising Knowledge vs Examining Reasoning

**Examining Reasoning** focuses on analyzing the quality of the thinking.  
**Revising Knowledge** focuses on the learner changing their understanding because the analysis, evidence, or feedback justifies a change.

The two often form a sequence:

```text
examine reasoning → identify weakness/new evidence → revise understanding
```

### Revising Knowledge vs Feedback

Feedback may trigger revision, but feedback itself is a classroom condition. The revision is the student's cognitive change in response to evidence or feedback.

## Computer science example

**Topic:** password security

A student begins with the belief that a complex-looking password is automatically secure. After examining password length, entropy, reuse, credential stuffing, and password manager practices, the student revises the claim to distinguish password complexity from overall credential security.

Strong evidence would show the original claim, the revised claim, and the reasoning or evidence that caused the change.

## Adaptation questions

If students keep the same misconception despite new evidence:

- Do they recognize the contradiction?
- Is the new evidence understandable and credible to them?
- Can they compare their original model directly with the new evidence?
- Would a counterexample or prediction test make the conflict clearer?
- Are they changing words without changing the underlying mental model?

## Defense prompts

- What prior understanding were students expected to reconsider?
- What evidence showed that their knowledge actually changed?
- What caused the revision: evidence, reasoning, feedback, comparison, or something else?
- How would you distinguish genuine revision from simply accepting the teacher's correction?
