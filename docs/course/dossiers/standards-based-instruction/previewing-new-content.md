# Previewing New Content

**Domain:** Standards-Based Instruction  
**Current status:** Publicly verified element name; public MEC example includes focus/desired-effect wording  
**Primary sources:** `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-DELIBERATE-PRACTICE`, `MEC-FTEM-CURRENT`

## Verified public-source claims

Current MEC material places **Previewing New Content** in the *building foundational knowledge* group. The public elements article describes the teacher engaging students in previewing activities that require access to prior knowledge related to upcoming content, with student evidence showing a connection between what they already know and what they are about to learn.

MEC deliberate-practice guidance also uses Previewing New Content as an example of a strategy a teacher might intentionally practice until it becomes fluent.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

Previewing is a **bridge-building move**. It is not a mini-lecture about the new content and it is not a review for its own sake. The purpose is to activate or surface knowledge that will help students attach new ideas to an existing mental structure.

The key diagnostic question is: **Did students make a useful connection to the upcoming learning?**

## Strong evidence

Useful evidence may include students:

- explaining a relevant prior concept before new instruction begins;
- predicting how an existing idea may relate to the new topic;
- producing a concept map, quickwrite, sketch, analogy, or discussion that reveals usable prior knowledge;
- revising an initial prediction after learning and explaining the connection.

## False positives

Do not assume previewing worked because:

- the teacher showed an engaging video;
- students answered a warm-up question unrelated to the new target;
- students discussed yesterday's lesson without connecting it forward;
- the teacher told students what they would learn;
- students were excited by a hook.

A hook may motivate. Previewing requires a meaningful connection from prior knowledge into the new content.

## Common confusion

### Previewing New Content vs Reviewing Content

**Previewing** looks forward and activates relevant knowledge for upcoming learning.  
**Reviewing** revisits previously learned content to strengthen or deepen it.

The same activity format could serve either purpose. Timing and instructional intent matter.

## Computer science example

**Upcoming topic:** subnet masks  
Before introducing masks, students annotate an IPv4 address and explain what they already know about binary place value and network/host separation.

The evidence is not that they completed the warm-up. The evidence is that their responses establish a usable connection between binary representation and the subnetting concept they are about to learn.

## Adaptation questions

If prior knowledge is weak, inaccurate, or disconnected:

- Which prerequisite is missing?
- Is the prior knowledge present but not being retrieved?
- Should I provide a brief example, analogy, or visual cue?
- Do I need to repair a misconception before proceeding?
- What evidence will tell me the bridge is now strong enough to continue?

## Defense prompts

- Why was this a preview rather than a review?
- What prior knowledge did students need for the new content?
- How did you know students actually connected the two?
- What would you do if the preview exposed a major prerequisite gap?
