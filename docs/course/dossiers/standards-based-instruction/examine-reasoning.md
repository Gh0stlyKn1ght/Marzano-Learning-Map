# Helping Students Examine Their Reasoning

**Domain:** Standards-Based Instruction  
**Current status:** Publicly verified element name and placement in extending thinking/analysis  
**Primary sources:** `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-CLASSROOM-CONDITIONS`, `MEC-FTEM-CURRENT`

## Verified public-source claims

Current MEC material places **Helping Students Examine Their Reasoning** in the *extending thinking / analysis* group of Standards-Based Instruction. A current MEC classroom-conditions article also describes rigorous learning as including students examining reasoning, debating conclusions, solving problems, and creating solutions.

The exact current licensed focus statement and desired effect are not established in the public sources used here.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

This element shifts attention from **the answer** to **the quality of the thinking that produced the answer**.

Students examine assumptions, evidence, logic, consistency, counterexamples, sources, or decision criteria. The point is not merely to explain a conclusion but to inspect whether the reasoning supporting it holds up.

## Strong evidence

Useful evidence may include students:

- identifying an unsupported assumption;
- checking whether evidence actually supports a claim;
- finding a flaw in a logical chain;
- testing a conclusion against a counterexample;
- comparing two arguments and explaining which is stronger;
- revising a justification after noticing a weakness;
- defending a conclusion while acknowledging limitations.

## False positives

Do not automatically count these as examining reasoning:

- debugging a syntax error with no analysis of underlying reasoning;
- explaining the steps used to reach an answer without evaluating them;
- saying “I agree” or “I disagree” without justification;
- correcting an answer because the teacher revealed the solution;
- participating in debate where claims are unsupported.

## Common confusion

### Examining Reasoning vs Elaborating on Content

**Elaborating** extends understanding through explanation, connection, inference, and detail.  
**Examining Reasoning** evaluates the quality of the reasoning itself.

### Examining Reasoning vs Revising Knowledge

Examining reasoning is the analysis process. **Revising Knowledge** is the change in the learner's understanding that may follow when new evidence or analysis warrants revision.

## Computer science example

**Topic:** algorithm choice

A student claims that binary search is “always faster” than linear search. The class examines the assumptions behind the claim: Is the data sorted? What is the collection size? What is the cost of sorting? Is random access available?

Students then evaluate whether the original claim is defensible under different conditions.

The core evidence is not that students know Big-O notation. It is that they can inspect the reasoning and identify where the claim does or does not hold.

## Adaptation questions

If students defend weak reasoning:

- Do they know what counts as evidence for this claim?
- Would a counterexample expose the assumption?
- Can I ask them to identify what must be true for the conclusion to hold?
- Would comparing two competing explanations make the reasoning visible?
- Are students evaluating the argument or merely searching for the teacher's preferred answer?

## Defense prompts

- What reasoning were students asked to examine?
- What made the task analytical rather than simply explanatory?
- What evidence showed students noticed weaknesses or assumptions?
- How did you respond when students reached a correct answer through weak reasoning?
