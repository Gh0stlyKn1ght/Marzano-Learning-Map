# Using Questions to Help Students Elaborate on Content

**Domain:** Standards-Based Instruction  
**Current status:** Publicly verified element name and cognitive-demand placement  
**Primary sources:** `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-FTEM-CURRENT`

## Verified public-source claims

Current MEC material places **Using Questions to Help Students Elaborate on Content** in the *building foundational knowledge* group. The current public article emphasizes that Standards-Based Instruction should place the intellectual work on students rather than reduce instruction to teacher delivery.

The exact current licensed focus statement and desired effect are not exposed in enough detail in the public sources used here, so this dossier does not invent them.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

Elaboration uses questioning to push students beyond a first-pass understanding. The teacher is not merely checking whether students remember a fact. Questions prompt students to explain relationships, infer, justify, extend, connect, or add detail to what they know.

The key distinction is **depth of explanation**. A question can be grammatically open-ended and still be cognitively shallow.

## Strong evidence

Useful evidence may include students:

- explaining why a concept works rather than only naming it;
- connecting a new idea to another concept or example;
- supporting an answer with reasoning or evidence;
- making a defensible inference;
- extending an explanation after a follow-up question;
- correcting an incomplete explanation because questioning exposed the gap.

## False positives

Do not automatically count these as elaboration:

- asking many questions rapidly;
- asking only recall questions;
- the teacher answering their own questions;
- one high-performing student doing all the explaining;
- asking “why?” without giving students time or structure to reason;
- calling on students until someone supplies the expected wording.

## Common confusion

### Elaborating vs Processing New Content

**Processing** helps students make initial sense of new information.  
**Elaborating** extends that understanding by requiring richer reasoning, relationships, inference, or justification.

### Elaborating vs Examining Reasoning

Elaboration expands understanding. **Examining Reasoning** more explicitly turns attention toward the quality, validity, logic, or support of the reasoning itself.

## Computer science example

**Topic:** recursion

After students trace a recursive function, the teacher asks:

- Why does the base case stop the recursive chain?
- What would happen if the base case were unreachable?
- How is the call stack changing at each step?
- Why might recursion be clearer here than an iterative solution?

Evidence comes from students extending and supporting their explanations, not from the number of questions the teacher asks.

## Adaptation questions

If responses remain shallow:

- Are my questions asking for reasoning or only recall?
- Do students need wait time or partner rehearsal?
- Do they have enough foundational knowledge to elaborate accurately?
- Would asking for evidence, a counterexample, a comparison, or a prediction deepen the response?
- Am I accepting the first plausible answer too quickly?

## Defense prompts

- What made these questions elaborative rather than simple checks for recall?
- What changed in students' explanations after your follow-up questions?
- How did you ensure more than a few students were doing the thinking?
- What would you do if questioning exposed that students lacked the foundational understanding needed to elaborate?
