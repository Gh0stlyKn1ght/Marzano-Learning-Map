# Using Formative Assessment to Track Progress

## Status

**Current public-source baseline established. Protocol-depth verification still open.**

## Verified public-source claims

Current Marzano Evaluation Center material places this element in the Conditions for Learning domain under **Monitoring Student Progress and Feedback**. Current MEC commentary emphasizes that formative assessment should not be something done only *to* students. Students should have an active role in tracking progress toward learning targets, with self-reflection, goal-setting, and progress tracking supporting ownership of learning.

Sources: `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-CLASSROOM-CONDITIONS`.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

The useful distinction is:

> **Formative assessment is the evidence-gathering and progress-tracking system, not simply the act of giving a quiz.**

A formative check becomes instructionally useful when it helps the teacher and/or student understand current position relative to the intended learning and informs what happens next.

## Strong evidence

Original examples:

- Students can explain where they currently are relative to a stated learning target.
- A quick code-trace check reveals which part of a loop students misunderstand, and the next task changes accordingly.
- Students use a clear criterion to compare current work with the target and identify a next step.
- Evidence is collected during learning rather than only after instruction has ended.

## False positives

These alone are not sufficient:

- giving a quiz,
- collecting an exit ticket that is never examined,
- posting a score,
- asking “Does everyone understand?”,
- using an online polling tool because it looks interactive,
- recording grades without using the information to understand progress.

## Closest neighbor

### Formative Assessment vs Feedback

**Formative assessment** primarily concerns gathering/interpreting evidence of current progress.

**Providing Feedback and Celebrating Progress** concerns what the learner receives or recognizes from that evidence and how progress is communicated or reinforced.

They often occur together, but they are not the same instructional act.

## Computer science example

Target: students can explain how a `for` loop changes state each iteration.

A teacher gives three short traces at increasing complexity. Students mark which trace they can explain confidently and identify where their reasoning breaks. The teacher notices most errors occur at the update expression and changes the next example to isolate that part.

The important part is not that a quiz existed. The important part is that evidence of progress became visible and informed subsequent learning.

## Adaptation questions

- What does the evidence show students can already do?
- Where exactly is the gap?
- Is the check aligned to the learning target or merely easy to score?
- Can students make sense of the evidence themselves?
- What changes next because of what we learned?

## Defense prompts

- Why was this check formative rather than simply another assignment?
- What did the evidence tell you that you did not already know?
- How did students use the evidence?
- What did you change because of the result?
- If nothing changed, what instructional purpose did the assessment serve?
