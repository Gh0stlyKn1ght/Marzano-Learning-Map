# Providing Feedback and Celebrating Progress

## Status

**Current public-source baseline established. Protocol-depth verification still open.**

## Verified public-source claims

Current Marzano Evaluation Center material places this element under **Monitoring Student Progress and Feedback**. MEC commentary emphasizes the teacher's role in providing feedback and recognizing progress while creating a classroom culture that values growth and improvement. Current classroom-conditions material also connects progress visibility with student motivation, persistence, ownership, and pride in accomplishment.

Sources: `MEC-ELEMENTS`, `MEC-CLASSROOM-CONDITIONS`.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

Useful feedback should help a learner answer at least one of these:

- Where am I relative to the target?
- What is working?
- What needs to change?
- What should I try next?

Celebrating progress is not empty praise. It makes meaningful improvement visible.

## Strong evidence

Original examples:

- Feedback points to a specific misconception in a network diagram and identifies the next revision.
- A student can explain how current code is stronger than the previous version and why.
- The teacher recognizes measurable improvement toward the learning target, not merely effort disconnected from learning.
- Students revise work in response to feedback.

## False positives

These alone are not sufficient:

- “Good job.”
- a grade with no actionable information,
- praise for speed,
- stickers or points unrelated to learning,
- correcting every error for the student,
- celebrating completion when the target was not met.

## Closest neighbor

### Feedback vs Formative Assessment

Formative assessment generates and interprets evidence of progress. Feedback communicates what that evidence means and supports the learner's next move. A teacher may gather excellent formative data but provide poor feedback, or give comments without a meaningful evidence base.

## Computer science example

A student's Python function works for the sample input but fails edge cases. Instead of saying “Almost there,” the teacher identifies that the condition assumes positive input and asks the student to test zero and negative values. After revision, the student compares both versions and explains the improvement.

## Adaptation questions

- Is the feedback specific enough to change the next attempt?
- Does it preserve student thinking, or did I solve the problem for them?
- Is progress being recognized against the target rather than against other students?
- Can the student explain what changed?

## Defense prompts

- What made your feedback actionable?
- What evidence showed that students used it?
- How did you celebrate progress without lowering expectations?
- Why was the feedback more useful than simply returning a score?
