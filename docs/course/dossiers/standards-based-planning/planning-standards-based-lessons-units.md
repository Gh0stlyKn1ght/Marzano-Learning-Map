# Planning Standards-Based Lessons/Units

## Status

**Current public MEC title confirmed.**

Current MEC material identifies this as the first Standards-Based Planning element and emphasizes alignment among grade-level standards, lesson/unit design, cognitive complexity, accurate content, and student tasks.

## Verified public-source claims

Current MEC material supports these points:

- planning begins with grade-level/content standards rather than disconnected activities,
- lesson/unit design should align the standard, learning target, task, and cognitive demand,
- task-target misalignment is a major instructional problem,
- students need opportunities to work at the cognitive level at which mastery will be expected.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

The planning problem is not "What activity should I do?" It is:

> What should students know or be able to do, at what level of thinking, and what task would actually require that thinking?

A plan is weak when the target asks for analysis but the task only asks for recall, or when the target expects creation/problem solving but the task is procedural completion.

## Strong planning evidence

Original examples:

- A networking standard requires students to analyze addressing constraints, and the planned task requires students to justify a subnet design rather than only calculate addresses.
- A programming lesson target asks students to compare algorithmic approaches, and the planned task requires comparison using runtime, readability, and constraints rather than writing one solution.
- A systems lesson expects students to explain process scheduling tradeoffs, and the task requires reasoning from a scenario rather than copying definitions.

## False positives

These do not establish strong standards-based planning by themselves:

- a standard pasted into a lesson plan,
- a learning objective displayed on the board,
- a list of activities,
- a difficult-looking assignment,
- extensive lesson notes,
- a project that takes several days.

The key question is whether target, task, content, and cognitive demand align.

## Nearest concepts

### Planning Standards-Based Lessons/Units vs Identifying Critical Content

Planning happens before instruction and establishes the target/task architecture. Identifying Critical Content is an instructional competency concerned with helping students recognize what is important during the learning experience.

### Planning vs Cognitively Complex Tasks

Planning may intentionally design for complex cognition. The Cognitively Complex Tasks element concerns the instructional use of such tasks and the student thinking/evidence produced during instruction.

## Adaptation questions

Before teaching:

- Is the task asking students to think at the same level as the target?
- What part of the task could allow students to succeed through a lower-level shortcut?
- Does the task require the content the standard actually values?
- What evidence will show the intended cognitive work occurred?

After teaching:

- Did student work reveal target-task misalignment?
- Was the task too low-level, too broad, or cognitively different from the target?
- What should change in the next lesson/unit plan?

## Defense prompts

- Why does this task actually match the cognitive demand of the standard?
- What would students have to do to demonstrate mastery rather than completion?
- Where could a student complete the activity without demonstrating the intended learning?
- If assessment evidence is weak, how would you know whether the issue was instruction or the original task design?
