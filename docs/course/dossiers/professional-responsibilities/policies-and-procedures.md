# Adhering to School and District Policies and Procedures

## Status

**Current element name verified. Public-source baseline established. Protocol-depth verification remains open.**

## Verified public-source claims

Current Marzano Evaluation Center material identifies **Adhering to School and District Policies and Procedures** as one of the three Professional Responsibilities elements.

The current FTEM page also places Professional Responsibilities within the four-domain, 23-competency teacher model and describes the model as evidence-based, growth-oriented, and tied to observable professional practice.

Public MEC material does not expose the full current protocol for this element. Teaching Practice Lab should therefore avoid claiming exact desired-effect wording, official evidence statements, or complete technique lists unless an authoritative current protocol becomes available.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

This element is about more than appearing compliant.

The professional problem is:

> A teacher works inside a larger public institution where some expectations are discretionary professional practice and others are binding legal, district, school, safety, ethical, or procedural requirements.

A strong teacher should be able to identify which kind of expectation is in play, follow the requirement, document appropriately when necessary, and seek clarification when a rule or procedure is unclear.

The core reasoning distinction is:

```text
professional preference
        ≠
binding requirement
```

and also:

```text
completing paperwork
        ≠ automatically
sound professional practice
```

## What useful evidence could look like

Original Teaching Practice Lab examples:

- required safety procedures are followed consistently in a robotics or electronics lab,
- grading/reporting procedures align with current school expectations,
- required accommodations or approved plans are implemented through the appropriate school process,
- attendance, supervision, assessment-security, technology-use, or incident procedures are followed when they apply,
- the teacher asks for clarification instead of inventing a workaround when policy is ambiguous,
- required documentation is accurate, timely, and limited to appropriate information.

These are examples, not official FTEM evidence statements.

## False positives

### "I completed the form."

A completed form does not prove the underlying responsibility was handled correctly.

### "I always do what I am told."

Professional responsibility is not mindless obedience. Teachers still need judgment, clarification, accurate interpretation, and ethical conduct.

### "My classroom is mine, so district procedures do not matter."

Classroom autonomy does not erase legal, safety, contractual, district, or school requirements.

### "Everyone else does it this way."

Informal custom is not automatically policy.

## Professional judgment boundary

Useful training scenarios should ask:

1. Is this a policy/procedure issue or an instructional-choice issue?
2. What source establishes the requirement?
3. What is mandatory versus locally discretionary?
4. What documentation is actually required?
5. When should the teacher ask an administrator, case manager, department lead, technology office, or other responsible role for clarification?

## CS / technology classroom examples

### Software installation

A teacher wants students to install a network tool on lab machines.

The instructional value may be strong, but the professional-responsibility question is separate:

- does district policy allow the software,
- does installation require administrator or IT approval,
- are privacy/security rules implicated,
- is the teacher authorized to alter the devices?

### Robotics lab safety

A student needs to troubleshoot powered hardware.

Knowing the technical procedure is not enough. The teacher must also follow applicable lab/safety procedures and supervision expectations.

### Student data

A teacher wants to use a cloud AI tool to analyze student work.

The question is not only whether the tool is educationally useful. Applicable district privacy, approved-software, account, data-retention, and student-information procedures matter.

## Adaptation / improvement questions

- Which procedures do I rely on from memory but should verify?
- Where am I using custom instead of written policy?
- Which recurring administrative task creates errors or delay?
- What checklist, calendar, or documentation habit would reduce preventable mistakes?
- Which policy areas change frequently enough that I need a recurring verification habit?

## Defense prompts

- How do you distinguish professional autonomy from a binding district requirement?
- What do you do when a procedure conflicts with what you think would be instructionally convenient?
- How do you respond when a colleague tells you, "We have always done it this way," but you cannot find the requirement?
- What evidence would show that your compliance is reliable rather than reactive?
- When should you escalate or seek clarification rather than make an independent decision?
