# Promoting Teacher Leadership and Collaboration

## Status

**Current element name verified. Strong public-source baseline available. Protocol-depth verification remains open.**

## Verified public-source claims

Current Marzano Evaluation Center material identifies **Promoting Teacher Leadership and Collaboration** as one of the three Professional Responsibilities elements.

Current MEC material describes teacher leadership and collaboration as central to a school functioning as a learning organization. Public material emphasizes teachers sharing responsibility for instructional practice, mentoring, collaborative problem-solving, common assessments, analysis of student learning, reflection on instruction, and observation/discussion of teaching and learning.

MEC also explicitly warns, in effect, that merely creating PLC structures does not guarantee useful collaboration. Productive collaboration requires clear purpose, thoughtful design, shared work, evidence, and attention to student learning.

## Teaching Practice Lab interpretation

This element is not:

```text
attending meetings
```

It is closer to:

```text
professional expertise
        ↓
shared with others
        ↓
collective instructional problem solving
        ↓
shared responsibility
        ↓
better organizational capacity
```

The professional problem is:

> A school cannot depend on isolated individual expertise. Strong professional practice contributes to the capability of colleagues, teams, departments, and the school as a whole.

Leadership here does not have to mean a formal title.

## Useful evidence could look like

Original Teaching Practice Lab examples:

- sharing an effective instructional approach and helping colleagues adapt it rather than merely forwarding a resource,
- co-designing common assessments and analyzing what student evidence means,
- mentoring a colleague through a specific instructional problem,
- leading a short technical workshop for a department,
- inviting observation and discussing what happened in the lesson,
- helping a PLC move from anecdote to evidence,
- taking responsibility for a shared curriculum or instructional problem,
- contributing reusable documentation, exemplars, or technical guidance that improves team capacity,
- facilitating disagreement productively around curriculum, assessment, or instruction.

These are original examples, not official FTEM evidence statements.

## False positives

### "I attend every PLC."

Presence is not collaboration.

### "I shared a Google Drive link."

Resource sharing may help, but leadership requires more than distribution.

### "I am department chair."

A title is not evidence of useful leadership.

### "Everyone agreed with me."

Consensus is not the goal. Productive collaboration can include disagreement, evidence, revision, and distributed responsibility.

### "I do everything because I am the expert."

Indispensability can actually reduce organizational capacity if others never develop expertise.

## Collaboration quality test

Teaching Practice Lab should train teachers to ask:

1. What shared instructional problem are we solving?
2. What evidence are we using?
3. What decision did collaboration change?
4. What responsibility is being shared?
5. Did someone else's capability increase?
6. How will we know whether the team decision improved learning or practice?

## CS / technology classroom examples

### Common technical baseline

Multiple CS teachers teach networking differently. The team identifies the essential concepts, compares student misconceptions, creates a common diagnostic, and agrees on evidence they will review after instruction.

### Shared lab safety

A robotics teacher develops a lab-safety process and then works with colleagues to refine and adopt it across courses rather than keeping it as a personal classroom system.

### Code review calibration

Programming teachers compare how they respond to student code, identify inconsistent expectations, and develop shared examples of what novice, developing, and strong reasoning look like without reducing evaluation to style preferences.

### Teacher observation

A colleague wants to improve questioning during debugging lessons. The teacher models a lesson, agrees on what the observer should watch for, and debriefs the evidence afterward.

## Leadership without title

Useful course scenarios should distinguish:

- formal leadership from influence,
- expertise hoarding from capacity building,
- meeting participation from collective problem solving,
- resource sharing from professional learning,
- collaboration from simple division of labor.

## Adaptation / improvement questions

- Where am I contributing expertise but not building anyone else's capacity?
- Which recurring team conversation produces little actionable evidence?
- What instructional problem would benefit from collaborative inquiry?
- What could I invite a colleague to observe?
- What common student evidence should our team examine together?
- When do I need to lead, and when should I deliberately create space for someone else to lead?

## Defense prompts

- Give an example of teacher leadership that does not require a formal title.
- How do you know collaboration improved something rather than simply consuming meeting time?
- What is the difference between sharing a resource and building colleague capacity?
- Describe a disagreement with colleagues that improved the final instructional decision.
- How do you prevent collaborative work from becoming compliance theater?
- What evidence would show that your leadership strengthened the team rather than making the team dependent on you?
