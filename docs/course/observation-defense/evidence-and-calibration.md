# Evidence and Calibration

## Why evidence quality matters

Current MEC material repeatedly frames FTEM around specific student evidence rather than impressionistic judgments about teacher activity. The current FTEM overview states that every competency is tied to a desired effect and observable student/teacher evidence, and that the final observation judgment is based on student evidence.

Teaching Practice Lab should therefore train the difference between:

- **observation**: what was directly seen or heard,
- **artifact**: work product, response, task, or other record,
- **inference**: what the evidence may indicate,
- **judgment**: the conclusion drawn from the evidence.

These should not be collapsed into one statement.

## Evidence ladder

A useful internal hierarchy is:

```text
weak
surface behavior
"students were quiet"

better
observable participation
"students completed the comparison prompt"

stronger
evidence tied to cognition
"students explained why the two routing approaches differ"

strongest for the claim
student evidence directly tied to the intended desired effect
```

The point is not that quiet behavior or participation is useless. The point is that it may not support the specific instructional claim being made.

## Percentage and distribution

The current FTEM overview publicly asks observers to determine what percentage of students demonstrate achievement of the desired effect and uses 91-100% student evidence as the threshold for Innovating.

Teaching Practice Lab should train percentage language cautiously:

- avoid unsupported precision,
- distinguish a sample from the whole class,
- notice who is not represented in the evidence,
- do not assume responses from a few volunteers represent everyone,
- state uncertainty when the available evidence is incomplete.

Example:

```text
Weak claim:
"The class understood recursion."

Stronger claim:
"Seven of the nine students whose explanations I heard could correctly explain the base case. I do not yet have evidence for the rest of the class."
```

## Calibration and inter-rater agreement

Current MEC implementation material emphasizes observer certification, calibration, scoring consistency, and inter-rater agreement. This is important because an evidence-based rubric still produces unreliable judgments when observers use different interpretations of the evidence.

Teaching Practice Lab can teach the *idea* of calibration without pretending to certify observers.

### Calibration exercise structure

Two people receive the same classroom case and independently record:

1. observable evidence,
2. likely relevant competency,
3. desired effect being examined,
4. evidence supporting or weakening the claim,
5. information still missing,
6. whether an adaptation occurred,
7. questions they would ask before making a stronger conclusion.

Then compare reasoning.

The goal is not merely to agree. The goal is to identify why interpretations diverged.

## Common evidence errors

### Halo effect

A strong general impression of the teacher influences unrelated judgments.

### Activity substitution

The observer treats the presence of a strategy as proof of its effect.

### Volunteer bias

A few vocal students are treated as representative of the class.

### Compliance bias

Quietness, speed, or orderly behavior is treated as evidence of understanding.

### Outcome hindsight

A correct final answer is treated as proof that the underlying reasoning or instructional process was sound.

### Overprecision

The observer reports exact-looking percentages that the evidence did not actually support.

## Course exercise: Evidence or inference?

Classify each statement:

> "Students were engaged."

Inference unless supported by defined observable evidence.

> "Six groups produced different subnetting plans and each group explained its tradeoff."

Observation/artifact description.

> "Students understood CIDR notation."

Inference unless tied to evidence demonstrating that understanding.
