# Instructional Defense Question Bank

This question bank is original Teaching Practice Lab material informed by current public FTEM observation, coaching, and deliberate-practice concepts.

It is designed for oral or written practice. Questions should increasingly remove framework vocabulary so the teacher must reason from the lesson itself.

## Level 1 — Explain the lesson

- What were students expected to learn?
- Why did you choose this task?
- What did you want students to do cognitively?
- What evidence were you looking for?
- How did you know whether students understood?
- What did you do when they did not?

## Level 2 — Evidence challenge

- What evidence supports that conclusion?
- How many students were represented in that evidence?
- What evidence would have changed your conclusion?
- Are you describing what students learned or simply what they did?
- What would count as a false positive here?
- What information was missing?
- Could a reasonable observer interpret the evidence differently? Why?

## Level 3 — Strategy challenge

- Why this strategy rather than another one?
- What instructional problem was the strategy solving?
- Which part of the task actually required the target thinking?
- If the activity were removed, what would students lose instructionally?
- What nearby FTEM competency could this be confused with?
- Why is your interpretation stronger than that alternative?

## Level 4 — Adaptation challenge

- What sign told you the original approach was not working?
- What did you believe the barrier was?
- Why did your adaptation address that barrier?
- What did you monitor after making the change?
- Did the evidence improve?
- If it did not improve, what would your next move be?

## Level 5 — Ambiguity challenge

- Do you have enough evidence to make that claim?
- What would you need to observe before deciding?
- Which students are missing from the evidence?
- Is this evidence of learning, engagement, compliance, or something else?
- What are two plausible explanations for what you observed?
- Which additional evidence would discriminate between them?

## Level 6 — Administrator mode

- How does this lesson align to the standard and intended cognitive demand?
- What was the most important instructional decision you made?
- What evidence shows that decision helped students?
- Where did students struggle?
- What did you change in response?
- What would you do differently if you taught the lesson tomorrow?
- How would you explain this lesson to someone who thought the activity was the objective?

## Level 7 — Skeptic mode

- You say students were engaged. What exactly did they demonstrate?
- You say the groups collaborated. How was that different from dividing the work?
- You say students understood. What evidence rules out simple answer-copying or guessing?
- You say the task was rigorous. What made the thinking complex rather than merely time-consuming?
- You say you differentiated support. How did you preserve the intended learning target?
- You say your feedback helped. What changed after students received it?

## Level 8 — Framework discrimination

Use only after the learner knows the elements well.

- Previewing or Reviewing? Defend the distinction.
- Processing or Elaborating? What student evidence separates them?
- Reviewing or Practice? What is the instructional purpose of each?
- Interaction with Content or Engagement? Could both be present?
- Examining Reasoning or Revising Knowledge? What changed in student thinking?
- Formative Assessment or Feedback? What is being gathered versus what is being communicated?
- Engagement or Cognitively Complex Tasks? What makes the task cognitively demanding?
- Relationships or High Expectations? What evidence belongs to each claim?

## Level 9 — Full defense

Prompt:

> Walk me through the lesson from standard to evidence. Explain the strategy choices, the desired student effects, the evidence you collected, what you could and could not conclude, any adaptations you made, and what you would do next. I will interrupt when a claim is unsupported.

The examiner should challenge:

- vague language,
- unsupported claims,
- element-name guessing,
- activity/evidence confusion,
- overgeneralization from a few students,
- unexplained adaptations,
- post-hoc rationalization.

## Strong-answer pattern

A strong answer often naturally contains:

```text
intent
→ mechanism
→ desired student response
→ evidence
→ limits of evidence
→ diagnosis
→ adaptation
→ new evidence
→ next step
```

The learner should not be required to recite this exact sequence. It is an evaluation aid for the Teaching Practice Lab practice system, not an FTEM scoring rubric.
