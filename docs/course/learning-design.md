# Learning Design for Teaching Practice Lab

Teaching Practice Lab is designed to produce **usable instructional fluency**, not familiarity with terminology.

The course should make a learner able to:

```text
understand → retrieve → distinguish → apply → diagnose → adapt → defend → transfer
```

This sequence is a **Teaching Practice Lab design model**. It is not an official Marzano developmental scale.

## Why the course is not built around reading alone

Repeated exposure can make framework language feel familiar without making it easy to retrieve or use under pressure. The course therefore uses concise explanation followed by retrieval, comparison, scenario analysis, and defense.

The learning-science basis is documented in the source registry:

- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): retrieval/testing can improve delayed retention relative to additional study.
- Butler (2010): repeated testing can improve transfer to new inferential questions and contexts.
- Cepeda et al. (2006): distributed practice supports retention across longer intervals.

These findings justify a course structure that repeatedly returns to important concepts in new forms instead of treating a lesson as complete after one reading.

## The course unit

Every major concept or FTEM competency should eventually contain the following learning layers.

### 1. Orientation

Answer:

- What is this concept?
- Where does it sit in the framework?
- What instructional problem is it trying to solve?
- What does the current source actually support?

### 2. Mechanism

Explain why the practice might matter instructionally.

Avoid strategy-name explanations such as:

> Use this because it is a Marzano element.

Prefer explanations that connect the instructional move to what students must think, do, produce, or revise.

### 3. Desired effect

Identify the student-side result that should become visible if the instructional move is working.

A desired effect is not:

- the teacher asking a question,
- students sitting in groups,
- a worksheet being completed,
- technology being used,
- the teacher displaying an objective.

Those may be activities or conditions. The important question is what changed in student understanding or performance.

### 4. Evidence

Ask:

> What observation would actually support the learning claim?

Course pages should distinguish:

- evidence that an activity occurred,
- evidence of compliance or participation,
- evidence of student thinking,
- evidence of student performance,
- evidence of transfer or revision.

The correct evidence depends on the desired effect. There is no universal evidence hierarchy that applies to every instructional claim.

### 5. False positive / non-example

Each concept needs at least one plausible classroom behavior that **looks like** the practice but does not establish that the intended effect occurred.

This prevents keyword matching and checklist thinking.

### 6. Contrast

For easily confused concepts, explicitly compare:

```text
purpose
+ timing / learning state
+ student cognitive behavior
+ expected evidence
```

The learner should be able to explain both:

1. why the selected concept fits,
2. why the plausible alternative fits less well.

### 7. Scenario

Move from definition to classroom reasoning.

A scenario should contain enough context to ask about:

- intent,
- strategy,
- desired effect,
- evidence,
- misconception/failure,
- adaptation,
- next evidence.

Later scenarios should deliberately contain ambiguity.

### 8. Adaptation

When evidence shows that the desired effect is weak, ask:

- What specifically appears to be wrong?
- What would you change?
- Why does that change address the diagnosed problem?
- What evidence will you collect after the change?

An adaptation is incomplete until its effect is checked again.

### 9. Retrieval

After the learner reads the page, the page should ask questions that can be answered with the explanation hidden or closed.

Useful retrieval prompts include:

- Define the practice in plain language.
- State its instructional purpose.
- Give an example.
- Give a non-example.
- What evidence would you want?
- What is it commonly confused with?

### 10. Defense

Each page should end with questions that resemble an instructional conversation rather than a vocabulary quiz.

Examples:

- Why did you choose this strategy here?
- What should I have seen students doing if it worked?
- How did you know it worked?
- What would have caused you to change the lesson?
- Why this move instead of the neighboring practice?
- What would you do differently tomorrow?

## Difficulty progression

### Beginner

The concept is named or strongly cued. Evidence is relatively clear.

Goal: establish accurate language and the causal reasoning loop.

### Practitioner

The concept is not named. Plausible neighboring practices are included. The learner must interpret evidence.

Goal: discrimination and application.

### Expert

The scenario may contain:

- incomplete evidence,
- mixed evidence,
- overlapping practices,
- a false positive,
- an unsupported conclusion,
- a need for adaptation,
- transfer to a new teaching context.

`Not enough evidence` must be a legitimate correct response when warranted.

Goal: calibrated professional judgment, not confident guessing.

## Spiral design

The course should not finish a concept and permanently leave it behind.

Later modules should bring earlier reasoning back through:

- mixed scenarios,
- comparison prompts,
- complete lesson analysis,
- defense questions,
- cross-domain cases.

Example:

```text
Foundations
   ↓
Instruction competency
   ↓
Conditions for Learning
   ↓
Mixed observation case
   ↓
Earlier instruction competency appears again
```

This is how retrieval and distributed practice become part of the course design without requiring an account or progress database.

## Real-practice extension

The platform can recommend optional real-classroom practice without storing student records.

Example:

```text
THIS WEEK

Focus:
Evidence vs Activity

Before class:
What student response will demonstrate the intended effect?

After class:
What did you actually observe?
What would you change next time?
```

No names, IDs, IEP details, grades tied to identities, or other student PII are required.

## The mastery test without a mastery tracker

Teaching Practice Lab no longer needs software to declare a learner “Master.”

A more useful standard is behavioral:

> Can the learner explain the concept accurately, recognize it without cues, distinguish it from neighboring concepts, apply it to a new classroom situation, diagnose weak evidence, choose an adaptation, and defend the decision under follow-up questioning?

If not, revisit the course material and practice again.
